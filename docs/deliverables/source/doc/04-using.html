Chapter 4Using AegisEO, step by step

This chapter follows a person through the website from the first page to a downloaded report, using real screenshots of the running application. What happens behind each step is explained briefly here and in depth in Chapters 9-13.

4.1 The five main pages

The top bar (a bottom tab bar on phones) always shows five destinations, named after what a person wants to do:

PageWhat it is for
HomeExplains AegisEO in one paragraph and offers four starting points; shows waiting review items, recent results and live alerts.
Check a placeThe four-step wizard that starts a new check.
ResultsEvery check ever run, newest first, with filters (needs a person, done, running, did not finish) and a search box.
To reviewEverything waiting for a person: findings to double-check and draft warnings to approve or reject. The orange number on the tab counts them.
AboutHow AegisEO works, its limits, the words it uses, how accurate it is, and where its data comes from.

The word check is the interface's name for one run of the analysis (in the code and the database it is called an analysis). A finished check is a result.

4.2 The home page

The four starting points lead into the same wizard with different parts filled in: Check a place (choose anywhere on a map), Explore a past disaster (five ready-made real case studies), Look at disasters happening now (current GDACS alerts) and Try a practice run (a made-up scene with known answers).

4.3 Step 1 - Where should AegisEO look?

The first step offers three ways to choose the area, shown as three tabs. A guide panel on the right explains the step, recaps every choice so far with a Change link, and says what comes next.

A past disaster

Each case study (defined in backend/aegis/scenarios.py) only says where and when to look; every image is still fetched live from the archive when the check runs. Their date ranges were chosen by hand so that suitable images exist; for Sindh, the "before" range (1 June - 5 July 2022) lies before the monsoon.

Happening now

An alert gives only one point, so choosing it draws a box of ±0.08° (about 18 km north to south) around that point. The area can then be moved with Change area.

Anywhere

An area must be between 0.25 km² and 1,500 km². The size badge turns orange above about 750 km² and explains that very large areas are processed at less detail; a disclosure below the map, "Why can one check cover at most 1,500 km²?", gives the reasons (Chapter 7). The limit shown is read from the server, so if an administrator changes it, the interface follows.

4.4 Step 2 - What happened there?

Earthquakes and storms are not listed: building damage and wind damage are smaller than these images can show. A storm's flooding can still be mapped by choosing Flood.

4.5 Step 3 - When did it happen?

The 'before' search and the 'after' search are date ranges in which AegisEO looks for images taken before and after the event. They can be changed under Change the search dates, which also explains what to do if no 'after' image is found (Figure 4.13). The optional Check which satellite images exist searches the real archive without downloading anything and lists the dates of every usable pass (Figure 4.14).

Impossible dates are explained and block the Continue button: a future date, a 'before' search ending after the event, or a search longer than 120 days. A 'before' and 'after' gap of more than 60 days only produces a warning.

4.6 Step 4 - Ready to start

Pressing Start the check sends the request to the server, which immediately replies with an identifier for the new check; the browser opens its result page and shows progress.

4.7 While the check runs

A real check usually takes 2 to 10 minutes the first time an area is checked (on this computer, first runs of 77-407 km² took 5 to 10 minutes, mostly spent downloading), and seconds when the same area is checked again because downloaded images are kept in a cache. A practice run takes a few seconds. Stop cancels a running check after its current step.

4.8 The result page

The top of the page answers the main questions before any detail: how much area changed and what share of the checked area that is, how many people normally live there (an estimate, not people hurt), how many important places are inside or within 500 m, and how many squares rank highest. A yellow box says how many items wait for a person, and Safety-check notes lists what the automatic checks reported (Figure 4.19).

The map

The map is drawn in the browser by MapLibre GL, a free mapping library. Its controls are: What's shown (turn layers on and off: priority squares, important places, villages and towns, the result layers, the satellite images, and expert layers such as model uncertainty and radar change), Expand map (fill the window), Compare before / after (a slider that reveals the 'before' image on one side and the 'after' image on the other, Figure 3.2), the background map choice (Map or Satellite), and a button to zoom back to the whole checked area. Clicking a square selects it in the list; clicking a place or a flooded patch shows its details.

The four tabs of the details panel

4.9 Results, To review and About

The About section also contains "Where the data comes from" (every source, its licence, limits and live status) and a plain explanation of the words used on the site. The search box titled "Have a question?" that used to appear on the About page was removed in this round of changes (Chapter 14).

4.10 Practice runs

Practice runs are generated by backend/aegis/synthetic.py: a made-up valley with a river, towns, crops and forest; simulated radar and optical images with realistic noise and clouds; and a known flood, burn or landslide footprint. They exist so that the system can be tested and demonstrated without the internet, and so that its accuracy can be measured against a known right answer. Their results are never presented as real.
